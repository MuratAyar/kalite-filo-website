## Calculate energy demand first

For each vehicle group, multiply expected daily distance by realistic consumption and add conversion losses. Identify the highest-demand days as well as the average. This produces an energy requirement; it does not yet determine charger count.

## Map dwell time and location

Record when vehicles arrive, depart and remain parked. Many vehicles can share fewer charge points when dwell windows are long and scheduling is controlled. Vehicles with short turnarounds may require higher power or dedicated capacity.

## Assess the electrical site

Have competent specialists review supply capacity, distribution equipment, cable routes, protection, metering and future expansion. Consider load management before assuming a supply upgrade is necessary. All design and installation must comply with current technical and safety requirements.

## Design the operating process

Decide access, vehicle-to-charger allocation, authentication, fault reporting, maintenance and out-of-hours support. Define public or home charging as a contingency and establish an auditable reimbursement method where relevant.

## Select data and controls

Useful reporting includes energy by vehicle, failed sessions, charger availability and peak demand. Integrate only the data needed for cost allocation and operation, with appropriate security and privacy controls.

## Pilot and expand

Test a representative group across seasonal and high-demand conditions. Review whether vehicles depart with the required energy and how often contingency charging is used. Reserve physical and electrical capacity for growth, then expand using measured demand rather than a fixed charger-to-vehicle ratio.
