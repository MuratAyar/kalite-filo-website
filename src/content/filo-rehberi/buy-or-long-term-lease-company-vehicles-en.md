## Define the decision correctly

Buying provides ownership of an asset; long-term leasing procures the use of a vehicle and an agreed service package. The comparison should cover the same vehicle, time horizon, mileage and operational requirement.

## Capital and cash flow

Purchase normally requires capital or financing and leaves the business exposed to the vehicle's future disposal value. Leasing converts much of the requirement into scheduled payments, but creates contractual commitments and may include charges when assumptions change.

## Operational workload

Ownership gives the company direct control over service providers and disposal timing, while also requiring internal capacity for maintenance, tyres, breakdowns, claims and sale. A managed lease can consolidate these tasks. The value depends on the actual service scope and provider performance.

## Flexibility and risk

Owned vehicles can often be retained beyond the original planning period or sold, although market value is uncertain. Leases define term, mileage and return condition, which supports planning but can make early change expensive. Test growth, contraction and high-mileage scenarios before choosing.

## Tax and accounting

Tax and accounting treatment depends on current law, the contract and the organisation. Use qualified advisers and apply the same assumptions to both options; do not base the decision on a generic claim about tax advantage.

## Compare total cost and strategic fit

Calculate financing or rentals, energy, maintenance, tyres, insurance, administration, downtime and disposal or return costs. Then consider whether vehicle ownership is strategically valuable to the business. A mixed policy may be appropriate where different roles have different utilisation and risk profiles.
