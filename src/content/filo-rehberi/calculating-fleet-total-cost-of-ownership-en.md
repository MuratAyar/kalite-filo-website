## Why monthly price is not enough

Total cost of ownership (TCO) measures every material cost created by a vehicle over the period in which the business uses it. Purchase price or monthly rental is only the starting point. Financing, taxes, insurance, fuel or electricity, maintenance, tyres, damage, administration and downtime can change the result substantially.

## Build a consistent cost model

Choose a common period and distance, such as 36 months and the expected kilometres. Record fixed costs separately from distance-related and event-driven costs. For owned vehicles, include capital cost and expected disposal value. For leased vehicles, include rentals, contract services, excess-mileage exposure and likely return-condition charges.

## Account for energy and maintenance

Use actual route and driver data where available. Manufacturer consumption figures are useful for comparison but should be stress-tested against urban traffic, payload, weather and driving style. Maintenance and tyre assumptions should reflect annual mileage and operating conditions rather than a generic average.

## Put downtime into the calculation

A vehicle that cannot perform its task may create replacement transport, delayed work, lost revenue and employee time. Estimate both the frequency and business impact of planned and unplanned downtime. This often reveals a difference between a low invoice cost and a genuinely efficient fleet.

## Compare scenarios, not a single forecast

Prepare a base case plus higher-mileage, higher-energy-price and early-exit scenarios. Use the same tax assumptions and service scope for every option. TCO is most useful as a repeatable decision method: update the model with actual costs, investigate variances and use the findings in the next vehicle and contract cycle.
